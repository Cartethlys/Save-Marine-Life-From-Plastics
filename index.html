Save Marine Life from Plastic

Plastic waste enters the sea every day. This site explains where it comes from, how it harms sea animals, and what can be done about it.

About this website

This website looks at one problem: plastic pollution in the ocean. It follows the problem from start to finish. First, how plastic gets into the sea. Second, what it does to animals and habitats. Third, what people, governments, and communities can do to reduce it.

The topic falls under SDG 14: Life Below Water, which is about protecting oceans and the living things in them.

Why it matters

Sea animals are at risk

Turtles, seabirds, fish, and corals are harmed when they eat plastic, get trapped in it, or lose habitat to it.

Swallowed plastic can block an animal's gut or leave it feeling full, so it eats less real food. Animals can also be injured when they are tangled in nets, ropes, or rings.

It does not go away

Plastic stays in the environment for hundreds of years. It breaks into microplastics rather than disappearing.

Sunlight and waves break plastic into smaller and smaller pieces, but most of it does not fully biodegrade. Estimates of how long plastic lasts vary a lot with the type of plastic and the conditions, so different sources give different numbers.

It can reach people

Microplastics have been found in seafood and seawater. Scientists are still studying the effects on human health.

Microplastics are plastic pieces smaller than 5 mm. Because they enter the food chain, people who eat seafood can take them in. Researchers are still working out what this means for health.

This estimate comes from a study of 192 coastal countries. It gave a range of 4.8 to 12.7 million tonnes, and 8 million is the middle of that range. A later OECD report estimated 1.7 million tonnes flowed into the ocean in 2019, so estimates differ depending on the method and year.

Sources: Jambeck et al. (2015); OECD Global Plastics Outlook (2022).

The Problem

Plastic is cheap and durable, and it is used once and thrown away. When it is not collected properly, it moves from land into rivers and then into the sea. Hover over a box to see more information.

8 million
tonnes of plastic estimated to have entered the ocean from land in 2010 (Jambeck et al., 2015)

The study estimated waste from people living within 50 km of the coast in 192 countries, then worked out how much was likely to be mismanaged and reach the sea. The range was 4.8 to 12.7 million tonnes. Other studies use different methods, so their numbers differ.

Source: Jambeck et al. (2015), Science.

Hundreds
of years plastic can persist in the environment

Plastic does not rot like food or paper. Instead it is broken by sunlight and waves into microplastics, which stay in the water and can be eaten by small organisms.

Rivers
carry a large share of land-based trash to the sea

Rivers connect towns, farms, and drains to the coast. Plastic that enters a river can travel a long way before it reaches the sea, and some of it gets stuck along the riverbanks and bottom.

Where ocean plastic comes from

Litter on land

Bags, bottles, and food packaging dropped on streets and drains get washed away by rain.

Street drains and gutters connect to rivers. In heavy rain, litter that was dropped far from the sea can be carried along them to open water.

Rivers

Rivers carry trash from towns and farms down to the coast and out to sea.

The OECD estimates that far more plastic has built up in rivers than in the ocean. This means plastic will keep reaching the sea for decades, even if less new waste is mismanaged.

Source: OECD Global Plastics Outlook (2022).

Fishing gear

Lost or abandoned nets, lines, and traps keep catching sea life for years. These are called ghost nets.

Fishing gear can be lost in storms, snagged on rocks, or thrown away. Once it is in the water it can keep trapping fish, turtles, and other animals, and it does not break down quickly.

Poor waste collection

Where rubbish is not collected, plastic is more likely to be burned, buried, or dumped near water.

Of the 353 million tonnes of plastic waste produced in 2019, about half went to landfill and only 9% was recycled. About 22% was dumped, burned in the open, or leaked into the environment.

Source: OECD Global Plastics Outlook (2022).

Why it does not go away

Most plastic does not fully biodegrade. Sunlight and waves break it into smaller pieces called microplastics, which can remain in the water for a long time.

How Plastic Harms Marine Life

Sea animals are harmed in several ways. Hover over a box to see more information.

Eating plastic

Sea turtles can mistake plastic bags for jellyfish, their natural food, and swallow them. This can block the gut or cause starvation.

Leatherback turtles mainly eat jellyfish, so floating bags and balloons can look like food. A turtle with plastic in its gut may not be able to absorb nutrients properly.

Entanglement

Fish, seals, and birds can get trapped in nets, ropes, and plastic rings, which can injure them or stop them from feeding and moving.

Turtles and seals breathe air, so an animal caught below the surface can drown. Ropes and rings can also cut into the skin as an animal grows.

Microplastics

Tiny plastic pieces are eaten by small organisms and move up the food chain to larger fish.

Microplastics are pieces smaller than 5 mm. Some come from larger plastic that breaks apart, and some are released directly, for example from synthetic clothes, vehicle tyres, and industrial plastic pellets.

More effects

Seabirds

Seabirds swallow bottle caps, lighters, and plastic pieces. These can fill their stomachs, so they eat less real food.

Seabirds often pick plastic off the water surface because it looks like prey. Chicks can also be fed plastic by their parents.

Coral reefs

Plastic can scrape, smother, or trap corals. Reefs are homes for many fish, so damage here affects the whole ecosystem.

When plastic lies on a coral, it can block light and let disease enter wounds. Because many species depend on reefs for food and shelter, damage to the coral affects the animals living there.

Human health

Microplastics have been found in seafood and seawater. Scientists are still studying what this means for people.

Fishing and tourism also depend on a clean sea, so plastic pollution affects people's food and income, not only their health.

Sea turtles

Sea turtle conservation status

Five of the seven sea turtle species are listed as Vulnerable or Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List.

The hawksbill and Kemp's ridley are Critically Endangered. The loggerhead, leatherback, and olive ridley are Vulnerable. The flatback is Data Deficient, meaning there is not enough data to judge. The green turtle was reassessed as Least Concern globally in 2025, but some local populations are still threatened.

Source: IUCN Marine Turtle Specialist Group, Red List assessments.

What Can Be Done

Tick the actions you can take in your own daily life. Hover over a box to see more information.

  • Carrying your own bag and bottle means fewer single-use items that can end up as litter.

  • Small items like straws and cutlery are easy to lose and hard to recycle, so refusing them at the start is the simplest fix.

  • Only 9% of plastic waste worldwide was recycled in 2019, so clean, sorted plastic helps recycling work better.

  • Clean-ups remove plastic before it breaks into microplastics, and counting what you find gives you local data to use.

  • Rules such as bag charges or limits on single-use items change what shops and businesses give out, not only what individuals choose.

  • People often do not see where their rubbish ends up. Sharing one fact or one local example can change habits around you.

Larger solutions

Each box shows evidence on how well the method works. The numbers measure different things, and most come from projections, trials, or single studies, so read the text in each box rather than comparing bars between boxes.

Reduce at the source

Cut unnecessary single-use plastic and design products that are easier to reuse or recycle.

UNEP projected that reuse systems, such as refillable bottles, bulk dispensers, and deposit-return schemes, could cut 30% of plastic pollution by 2040. Replacing items like wrappers, sachets, and takeaway packaging with other materials could cut a further 17%. UNEP says all its measures together, starting with removing unnecessary plastic, could cut pollution by 80%. These are projections, not measured results.

Source: UNEP, Turning off the Tap (2023).

Better waste management

Reliable collection, sorting, and recycling keep plastic out of drains and rivers.

The OECD found that in non-OECD countries a much larger share of plastic waste is mismanaged or uncollected, and it calls for major investment in basic waste management in low and middle-income countries. UNEP projected that making recycling more stable and profitable, for example with design rules that make products easier to recycle, could cut a further 20% of plastic pollution by 2040.

Sources: OECD Global Plastics Outlook (2022); UNEP, Turning off the Tap (2023).

River trash traps

Barriers placed across rivers can catch floating rubbish before it reaches the sea.

Results depend on the design and the river. The developers of a bubble barrier reported that it caught 80% of test material in a trial in the Netherlands. First results from an Interceptor in Jakarta showed it could collect up to 60% of solid waste passing by. A study of one passive litter trap found it caught about 11% of the litter in the waterbody it targeted. Traps work better in well-chosen locations, and they only catch trash that is already in the river.

Sources: Great Bubble Barrier trial results; Dutch Water Sector (Interceptor results); CLEAR RIVERS litter trap study.

Fishing gear recovery

Programmes that collect and recycle old nets remove ghost gear from the ocean.

One 2023 cleanup in Norway recovered gillnets and king crab pots that had trapped 307 animals weighing 382 kg in total. Per kilogram of gear, the gillnets had caught 27.3% more animals and 50.3% more biomass than the crab pots. A 2007 cost-benefit study of European waters suggested that retrieval programmes may give less value for money than other measures, such as stopping gear from being lost in the first place.

Sources: Vodopia et al. (2024), Marine Pollution Bulletin; Brown and Macfadyen (2007), Marine Policy.

Policy

Plastic bag bans, bottle deposit schemes, and producer responsibility rules can reduce plastic on a large scale.

England introduced a 5p charge on single-use carrier bags in October 2015. Government figures show the average person bought about 140 bags a year from the main supermarkets in 2014 and about 2 a year in 2022 to 2023. This covers carrier bags only, which the OECD notes are a small share of all plastic waste. It shows that a policy can change behaviour quickly, but it does not solve plastic pollution by itself.

Sources: UK Government (2023); OECD Global Plastics Outlook (2022).
